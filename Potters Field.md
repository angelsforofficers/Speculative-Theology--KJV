We’re all familiar with Judas Iscariot accepting 30 pieces of silver, which was used to buy the Potter’s Field, but there are FAR more layers to the significance of this action than what’s immediately apparent, as well as a challenge for the believer today.

Hacksilver may come at various weights per individual piece, which is why they always used scales in the OT to determine the worth.

This is an interesting figure since the Lord sets a precedence for what this amount can buy in Exodus and Leviticus. And there are two things to gain from this.

**Zechariah 11:12-13**  
\[13\] And the LORD said unto me, Cast it unto the potter: a goodly price that I was prised at of them. And I took the thirty pieces of silver, and cast them to the potter in the house of the LORD.

- By paying Zechariah thirty pieces of silver, the people were valuing the Lord, and the “words of the Lord” as much at the minimal value of a middle aged person.  
- “Goodly price” is sarcasm.  
- He says to “cast them to the potter in the house of the Lord”

In the temple, there would often be a dedicated “Potter” by trade since in the Temple service, pottery wasn’t just for storage; it was a ritual necessity. Many offerings (such as grain, oil, and wine) were brought or processed in earthen vessels.

Under the Mosaic Law, these vessels had a "one-time-use" nature in certain contexts. For example…

**Leviticus 6:28**   
“But the earthen vessel wherein it is sodden shall be broken: and if it be sodden in a brasen pot, it shall be both scoured, and rinsed in water.”

Because earthen vessels were porous, they could absorb the "holy" or "unclean" elements and could not be ritually cleansed like metal. So they had to be smashed and replaced constantly.

This created a massive, ongoing demand for new pottery, requiring potters to be stationed nearby or even within the Temple precincts to provide a steady supply of ritual-grade vessels.

On the other hand, the Temple Treasury was where high-value gifts and "holy" money was kept. By casting the silver to the potter instead of into the treasury, Zechariah was performing a symbolic act of insult.

Instead of being accepted as a "holy" offering to God, the money was thrown to a common, dirty laborer. Doing this inside the Temple showed that even in God’s own house, the people valued Him no more than a common piece of breakable clay.

Now, while it was an insultingly low price for the life of the Messiah, it is enough of a "lump sum" to buy a small plot of real estate intended for a graveyard. Land not profitable for building houses, raising cattle or growing crops.
